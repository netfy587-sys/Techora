Wi-Fi, routers, DNS and speed tests can sound confusing. Techora explains the main ideas in plain language, so you can understand what is happening when you go online and what to check when something goes wrong.

What Happens When You Open a Website?

A page seems to appear instantly, but several steps happen in a fraction of a second. Here is a simplified version.

You Ask

You type an address or tap a link. Your device prepares a request for that page.

DNS Looks It Up

Names like a website address are converted into numeric addresses that computers use. This is the job of the Domain Name System.

Data Travels

The request is broken into small pieces called packets, which pass through your router, your provider and many networks along the way.

The Server Replies

The website's server receives the request and sends back the page's data, again in packets.

Your Browser Builds It

Your device reassembles the pieces, and your browser displays the text, images and layout on screen.

The Parts of a Typical Home Network

Home setups vary, but most include the same few building blocks. Sometimes several are combined in one box.

Modem

Connects your home to your internet provider's network and translates the signal into a form your equipment can use.

Router

Directs traffic between your devices and the internet, and gives each device a local address. Many routers also provide Wi-Fi.

Access Points and Extenders

Extra devices that help spread Wi-Fi to areas the main router does not reach well, such as upstairs rooms or garages.

Your Devices

Phones, computers, TVs, speakers and smart-home gadgets all share the connection, which can affect speed when many are active.

Getting Better Wi-Fi at Home

Wi-Fi uses radio waves, which can be weakened by distance, thick walls, metal and other electronics. Small changes in setup often make a noticeable difference.

Placement matters. Put the router in a central, open position, ideally off the floor and away from thick walls, metal objects and microwave ovens. Hiding it in a cabinet can reduce signal strength.

Understand the bands. Many routers offer two bands. The 2.4 GHz band usually reaches farther but is often slower and more crowded, while 5 GHz is generally faster over shorter distances. Choosing the right one for each device can help.

Consider a wired connection. For stationary devices that need steady performance, such as a desktop or streaming box, a network cable is often more reliable than Wi-Fi.

Key Terms in Plain English

A short glossary of words you will often see in settings and speed tests.

IP address A number that identifies a device on a network, like a mailing address for data.

DNS The system that turns website names into the numeric addresses computers use.

Bandwidth How much data a connection can carry at once. Higher bandwidth is like a wider road.

Latency The delay before data starts to arrive, measured in milliseconds. Low latency feels more responsive, which matters in video calls and gaming.

Packet A small chunk of data. Information is split into packets, sent separately and reassembled at the destination.

SSID The name of a Wi-Fi network as it appears in your device's list of networks.

Firewall A security feature that filters network traffic according to rules. Routers and devices often include one.

Mbps Megabits per second, the usual unit for internet speed. Real-world speeds can be lower than advertised for many reasons.

Simple Troubleshooting When the Internet Feels Slow

Try these steps in order before assuming something is seriously wrong. If problems continue, contact your internet provider.

1. Check the Basics

Confirm that cables are firmly connected and that the router and modem lights look normal. Check whether the problem affects one device or all of them.

2. Restart Equipment

Turn off the router and modem, wait about thirty seconds and switch them back on. This clears temporary glitches and often helps.

3. Move Closer

Test the connection near the router. If it improves, distance or obstacles may be the cause rather than the service itself.

4. Reduce Congestion

Large downloads, video streams and updates on other devices can use much of your bandwidth. Pausing them can show whether sharing is the issue.

5. Update and Restart Devices

Out-of-date software or a device that has run for a long time can behave oddly. Install pending updates and restart.

6. Test More Than Once

Speed tests vary with time, server and Wi-Fi quality. Run several, ideally on a wired connection, before drawing conclusions.

Safer Habits for Your Home Network

A few simple settings reduce common risks. No setup can guarantee complete security.

01

Change default passwords. Replace the router's default login and Wi-Fi password with strong, unique ones.

02

Use modern Wi-Fi security. Choose WPA2 or WPA3 where available and avoid open or outdated security modes.

03

Keep firmware updated. Router makers release updates to fix problems and security weaknesses. Enable automatic updates if offered.

04

Use a guest network. Visitors and smart gadgets can use a separate network, keeping your main devices apart.

05

Be careful on public Wi-Fi. Avoid entering sensitive information on open networks and prefer secure, encrypted connections.

06

Review connected devices. Occasionally check the list in your router's settings for devices you do not recognise.

What is the difference between a modem and a router?

A modem connects your home to your internet provider. A router shares that connection among your devices and often provides Wi-Fi. Some providers supply one device that does both.

Why is my internet slower than the speed I pay for?

Wi-Fi quality, distance, congestion, device limits and the website's own server can all reduce real-world speeds. Testing on a wired connection helps identify the cause.

Should I leave my router on all the time?

Most routers are designed to stay on. An occasional restart can clear temporary issues, and you should follow the manufacturer's guidance.

Is it safe to use public Wi-Fi?

It carries more risk than a private network. Avoid sensitive activities when possible, use secure websites and consider additional protections recommended by trusted sources.
